# World History Atlas - 8000 BCE-500 BCE

**Priority historical research collection | Research cut-off: 22 October 2026, Australia/Brisbane**

## Executive assessment

This collection follows changes in food production, settlement, communication, collective construction, specialised work and political institutions. Its organising question is not "which civilisation was most advanced?" but **which developments substantially changed how people lived, organised resources and related to other communities?** Selection is editorial, and regional significance counts alongside later worldwide influence.

The requested inclusive span is **8000 BCE through 500 BCE**: astronomical `[-7999, -498)`, containing **7,501 numbered years**. It is an authorised macro-period, not a 500-year block. The collection includes earlier or later context where necessary, with the original dates retained. A long-lived community or process has not been assigned artificial birth and death dates at the package boundaries.

The central finding is that there was no universal sequence in which farming automatically produced villages, then cities, then states. Jomon and Poverty Point provide different examples of substantial settlement or collective construction outside that simple sequence; Kuk and Gunditjmara evidence makes environmental engineering visible beyond the conventional western Asian narrative. These comparisons concern distinct evidence and do not imply contact between those societies. [src_jomon](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1632/) [src_poverty](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/monumental-earthworks-of-poverty-point-world-heritage-site.htm) [src_kuk](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/887/) [src_budj](https://www.budjbim.com.au/about-us/our-culture/)

This is a **selective, source-linked foundation**, not comprehensive coverage of seven and a half millennia. It contains 46 event/process/context records and 27 technology observations. Some are broad archaeological phases or relative-date context, not tightly dated historical events. The identity table does not claim independently researched lifespans for every culture or polity.

## 1. Food systems, settlement and different routes to continuity

The opening boundary is not the beginning of agriculture or human settlement. Jericho's Neolithic structures and settlement evidence extend into the ninth and eighth millennia BCE, while Catalhoyuk provides a later, unusually concentrated record of household-based life and repeated building. The two sites should not be collapsed into a single moment when "cities were invented". Their different settlement forms, occupations and archaeological contexts matter. [src_jericho](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1687/) [src_cat](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1405)

Food production itself needs several separate concepts: exploitation of wild plants, cultivation, selection for domestication traits, movement of a crop and later intensification. The Balsas maize observation belongs to early evidence of use and cultivation, not a precisely identified first domestication ceremony. A 2026 Lower Yangtze study likewise presents rice domestication as regionally varied, involving coexistence and changing proportions of wild and domesticated traits. These are process histories, not patent dates. [src_maize](https://repository.si.edu/items/0cedfac1-0d93-4b11-8573-de64560518d3) [src_rice2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-026-02510-x)

A particularly consequential revision concerns Mehrgarh. A 2025 study dated tooth enamel from 23 burials and modelled cemetery onset between **5200 and 4900 BCE**, substantially later than older early chronologies. The pack preserves the study's proposed sequence and marks the chronology disputed. It does not turn one site's revised cemetery model into proof that nobody cultivated crops anywhere in South Asia before that date. The relevant contrast is between attributable chronological models, not between an unquestionable old textbook and an unquestionable new paper. [src_mehrgarh](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-92621-5)

Migration and interaction were also uneven. Genomic research supports substantial incoming-farmer involvement in Britain's Neolithic transition, while work on the Rhine-Meuse region emphasises longer persistence and interaction among communities with different subsistence histories. Such evidence does not justify a universal rule of peaceful diffusion, wholesale replacement or a fixed ancestry-to-culture equivalence. [src_britain_farming](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-019-0871-9) [src_rhine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10111-8)

Kuk is included as a distinct wetland cultivation history in New Guinea. The heritage description distinguishes cultivation mounds and later drainage rather than one hydraulic invention. Its relative date language is preserved in an unplaced context record pending a phase-level calibration review. Gunditjmara kooyang aquaculture is also retained as long-duration, community-authorised context, with channels, weirs and holding ponds treated as a coordinated food system. No invented founding year, sensitive individual feature location or single continent-wide Indigenous state is supplied. [src_kuk](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/887/) [src_budj](https://www.budjbim.com.au/about-us/our-culture/)

## 2. Early urban centres were different kinds of societies

Southern Mesopotamian Ubaid settlements combined managed water, cultivation and other local resources. Later Uruk institutions supported much larger urban concentrations and increasingly specialised recording. This sequence gives the Atlas a way to connect settlement, labour and information without asserting that irrigation alone caused state formation. The institutional interpretation is broader than any one canal or tablet. [src_ubaid](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-ubaid-period-5500-4000-b-c) [src_uruk](https://www.metmuseum.org/ja/essays/uruk-the-first-city)

Elsewhere, early large settlements followed distinct histories. Liangzhu's approximately 3300-2300 BCE setting combines rice cultivation, earthen construction, water conservation and differentiated burials. Dholavira, occupied broadly around 3000-1500 BCE, demonstrates planned urban space and sophisticated water management in an arid environment. Caral-Supe's 3000-1800 BCE sequence provides an independently important Andean example of monumental platforms and courts. These are useful contemporary comparisons, not evidence of one global urban network. [src_liang](https://whc.unesco.org/fr/list/1592) [src_dholavira](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1645/) [src_caral](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1269/)

The Nile sequence combines increasingly centralised kingship, early written labels and later Old Kingdom stone construction. Monumental building is therefore connected to institutions and resource organisation, rather than presented as an isolated engineering trick. The later transition away from Old Kingdom centralisation is retained separately: changes in royal authority do not mean the disappearance of Egyptian populations or knowledge. [src_egypt](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/toah/ht/02/afe.html)

Poverty Point is a necessary corrective to a state-centred account. Its approximately 1700-1100 BCE earthworks were created and used by hunter-fisher-gatherer communities. Collective construction and extensive material exchange are therefore not, by themselves, proof of a cereal-farming empire. Likewise, the broad Jomon context illustrates substantial sedentary traditions associated with hunting, fishing and gathering. The Atlas should display the kind of community the evidence supports, not promote every archaeological complex to a kingdom. [src_poverty](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/monumental-earthworks-of-poverty-point-world-heritage-site.htm) [src_jomon](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1632/)

## 3. Records, materials and institutional memory

Early Mesopotamian writing grew within accounting and administrative practices. The reviewed museum account distinguishes early signs and rebus techniques from later, more extensive phonetic representation. The dataset therefore separates an accounting observation from a writing-system observation. It does not equate the oldest surviving tablet in a reviewed sample with the first human invention of writing. [src_writing](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/essays/the-origins-of-writing)

Written records later supported different institutions. Hammurabi's monumental collection associates royal authority with legal cases and judgements; it is not described here as the world's first law or as a modern comprehensive statute book. Ashurbanipal's collecting at Nineveh represents another use of writing: assembling, copying and preserving scholarly and literary knowledge. These records make better technology-tree nodes when their institutional purposes are visible. [src_hammurabi](https://www.louvre.fr/en/the-code-of-hammurabi) [src_nineveh](https://www.metmuseum.org/it/essays/nineveh)

Shang bronze production is represented through a regional casting tradition and its ritual setting, not a claim that all bronze casting originated in China. Olmec-region and Liangzhu craft records similarly concern specialised work, materials and social contexts. The Douroula observation concerns iron production in the eighth century BCE in present-day Burkina Faso; it is not promoted to the earliest iron smelting anywhere in the world or to proof of one particular transmission route. [src_shang](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/essays/shang-and-zhou-dynasties-the-bronze-age-of-china) [src_olmec](https://www.metmuseum.org/es/essays/olmec-art) [src_liang](https://whc.unesco.org/fr/list/1592) [src_douroula](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1602/)

The selected dependencies are deliberately modest. Economic recording is a documented institutional context for early Mesopotamian writing; water and land management enabled particular wetland cultivation at Kuk. Neither statement means that all writing required the same economic organisation or that all farming required irrigation. The dependency graph is contextual, not a universal civilisational ladder. [src_writing](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/essays/the-origins-of-writing) [src_kuk](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/887/)

## 4. Exchange, mobility and the reach of political power

Crop movement offers unusually informative evidence for connections between regions. A 2025 study reports two directly dated pearl-millet grains from Bhando Qubo in Sindh, with calibrated ranges of **2578-2358** and **2576-2356 BCE**. The finding pushes back secure South Asian evidence and links it to an African crop history. The receiving-site dates do not identify the sending polity, every intermediary or an exact voyage. Accordingly, the transmission record leaves the origin society null and contains no route geometry. [src_millet](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-20110-w)

Horse-based mobility is also handled cautiously. Genomic work associates a major expansion of particular managed horse lineages with the period around 2200 BCE. That does not establish the earliest riding or every earlier use of horses. The record is an approximate-date observation, not a fabricated one-year event or a claim that one horse tradition instantly transformed all Eurasia. [src_horse](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/11269178/)

Pacific maritime histories are represented through early Mariana settlement and Lapita-associated expansion. They establish successful ocean crossings and expanding settlement networks, but not precise unobserved sailing tracks. These records must not be back-projected onto much later settlement histories of remote Eastern Polynesia. In the Mediterranean, Phoenician coastal cities provide a different network model: related cities and maritime exchange, not one continuous sea-spanning territorial state. [src_mariana](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/re-envisioning-long-distance-oceanic-migration-early-dates-in-the/) [src_lapita](https://teara.govt.nz/en/map/1762/the-pacific-ocean-showing-remote-oceania) [src_phoenicia](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-phoenicians-1500-300-b-c)

The political records distinguish conquest, administration and cultural continuity. Akkadian expansion belongs to a broader Mesopotamian urban world. Hattusha illustrates the infrastructure supporting a capital, while Egyptian control in Upper Nubia coexisted with continuing Nubian communities. Napatan political and sacred centres remain visible as African history in their own right, rather than merely a southern extension of an Egyptian narrative. [src_akkad](https://www.metmuseum.org/ko/essays/the-akkadian-period-ca-2350-2150-b-c) [src_hattusha](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/377/) [src_nubia](https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/03/afs.html) [src_napata](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1073/)

At the later end of the span, the sack of Nineveh in 612 BCE and Achaemenid expansion under Cyrus and Cambyses changed imperial institutions across western Asia and Egypt. Darius's reign is retained beyond the 500 BCE endpoint because its administrative context cannot be understood by inventing a historical ending at the research boundary. None of these political transitions is coded as disappearance of an entire people. [src_nineveh](https://www.metmuseum.org/it/essays/nineveh) [src_persia](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-achaemenid-persian-empire-550-330-b-c)

## 5. Political and ethical ideas at the boundary

The collection includes the conventional 508 BCE date for Cleisthenic reforms at Athens as a significant institutional anchor. It does not reproduce the source's expansive claim that every modern representative democracy simply derives from Athens. Detailed citizenship exclusions, competing 508/507 dating and the subsequent development of institutions require further specialised treatment. [src_athens](https://www.namuseum.gr/en/collection/klasiki-periodos/)

Confucius appears as an intellectual-context record using traditional life dates of 551-479 BCE. The Stanford discussion stresses the variety of later representations and interpretations. The record therefore does not claim that the Analects was composed before 500 BCE or that a complete, unchanging doctrine can be dated to a single teacher's lifetime. The later life endpoint is preserved as context rather than clipped. Buddhist, Jain, Vedic, Levantine and Iranian intellectual histories remain explicit gaps instead of being filled with loosely remembered "Axial Age" assertions. [src_confucius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/)

## 6. Meanwhile elsewhere: useful comparisons

The comparison library contains 8 linked cards. Particularly useful pairings are **Liangzhu and Caral-Supe in the third millennium BCE**, **Old Kingdom construction and Dholavira's urban water systems**, and **Poverty Point and Shang specialised production**. A separate card compares different responses to incoming farming in Britain and the Rhine-Meuse region. Each card points to underlying evidence records. Contemporaneity, similarity and actual contact remain separate concepts. [src_liang](https://whc.unesco.org/fr/list/1592) [src_caral](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1269/) [src_egypt](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/toah/ht/02/afe.html) [src_dholavira](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1645/) [src_poverty](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/monumental-earthworks-of-poverty-point-world-heritage-site.htm) [src_shang](https://www.metmuseum.org/fr/essays/shang-and-zhou-dynasties-the-bronze-age-of-china) [src_britain_farming](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-019-0871-9) [src_rhine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10111-8)

These comparisons are editorial teaching devices. They do not claim that societies with broadly overlapping archaeological ranges were interacting, or that similar-looking infrastructure had a common inventor. The cards involving Kuk and Gunditjmara explicitly avoid exact simultaneity because the source wording currently supports relative chronology rather than a calibrated event date.

## 7. Geography, evidence quality and the largest remaining gaps

The map package contains **three public heritage-property anchors**, for Kuk, Caral-Supe and Dholavira. Public coordinates were converted from the UNESCO listings and rounded to three decimal places. They are navigation references, not surveyed historical boundaries, exact building locations or centres of entire cultures. There are **no original historical maps, reconstructed empire polygons or invented diffusion arrows** in this release. [src_kuk](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/887/) [src_caral](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1269/) [src_dholavira](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1645/)

Important omissions remain: mainland and island Southeast Asia, much of Central and Southern Africa, Central Asian settlement systems, additional American agricultural traditions, detailed Late Bronze Age transformations, most early religious traditions, and non-elite lives beyond a few archaeological cases. There is no defensible global population or city-size series here. Empty cells in the coverage matrix are labelled unresearched, not uninhabited or historically unimportant.

Newer studies improve the collection but do not abolish uncertainty. Mehrgarh chronology, rice domestication pathways, wheat domestication models and interpretations of genomic evidence need independent specialist evaluation. Heritage descriptions and museum syntheses are strong discovery and orientation sources, not substitutes for every excavation report. The quality file preserves these distinctions and the source register records the actual access level.
