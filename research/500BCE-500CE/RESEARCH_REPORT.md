# WORLD HISTORY ATLAS — Global Research Dossier, 500 BCE to 500 CE

**Research date:** 21 October 2026  
**Exact scope:** 500 BCE through 500 CE, both included, astronomical year interval `[-499, 501)`  
**Status:** Source-linked research seed prepared for review and staged Atlas import. **Not** a comprehensive or peer-reviewed database.

## Executive synthesis

These ten centuries saw the formation, expansion and transformation of political, religious, commercial and technological systems on several continents. The evidence is not evenly distributed: Mediterranean and Chinese writings allow fine political chronologies, while many American, African and Pacific developments are reconstructed from settlement archaeology, objects and landscape engineering. The Atlas must display both kinds of history without pretending that written dynastic lists make one society more historically significant.

The narrative cannot be a history of Rome with a few foreign sidebars. Around 250 BCE, the Mediterranean was occupied by competing Hellenistic kingdoms and a rising Roman Republic, while Mauryan states and Ashokan inscriptions shaped South Asia, Qin-era rulers contested China, maritime networks connected South Arabia and northeast Africa, and societies across the Americas developed independently. By 400 CE, Roman administration was changing in both east and west; the Sasanians had replaced Parthian dynastic power; Gupta court networks and regional South Asian kingdoms were active; Korean kingdoms and Yamato-period Japanese polities were changing; Aksum had developed coinage and royal Christianity; and substantial political and ceremonial centres flourished at Teotihuacan, Tikal, Monte Albán, Hopewell sites and along the Peruvian coast.

The research pack contains **153 society/state/archaeological-cultural records**, **83 events**, **36 technology topics and 63 dated technology observations**, **50 historical figures**, **41 relationship edges**, **30 approximate mapped place anchors**, **5 historical cartographic catalogue entries**, **25 cultural practices**, **12 comparative cards**, **61 source references** and **22 explicit research gaps**. Structural cross-reference checks passed, but most historical assertions are backed only by one broad scholarly or institutional overview. Treat the data as an expandable research foundation.

The companion report supplied to this research pass had 48 society records and 29 events. This expanded pack is **not** simply a copied report: it adds records and links across regions and preserves the previous work as a research seed. It does not silently promote that seed to independently verified scholarship.

**Overlap with the prior Atlas pack:** The existing 500–1000 CE research package uses `[500,1000)`, so year **500 CE is present in both research ranges**. This is deliberate here because the user requested 500 CE inclusive. Merge tools must use stable IDs and deduplicate that one-year overlap; the older 500–1000 CE pack should be the canonical owner for any identical date-500 record unless there is stronger new evidence. Do not clip a society to the research boundaries: Eastern Rome, the Sasanians, Aksum and many other identities continue past 500.

A date range is not a precision claim. ``c. 200–300 CE`` in a seed row means approximate evidence spanning a historical phase, not continuous use proven in every intermediate year. Dynasties and archaeological cultures are given approximate start and end values for timeline navigation, with separate confidence and precision fields. Real cities and archaeological sites have modern-point locators; political borders are **not** generated.

## 2. Chronological survey

### 500–401 BCE: Mediterranean competition and diverse early traditions

At the beginning of the interval, Achaemenid power extended through much of West Asia, Classical Greek poleis had different political systems, and the Mediterranean included Etruscan, Punic and other regional centres. The early Ionian revolt and subsequent wars conventionally dated from 499 BCE connect Greek maritime politics to Achaemenid imperial governance. The Athenian reforms often dated 508/507 BCE appear in the dataset strictly as **pre-window background** rather than an event inside this time block.

Across South Asia, the eastern Ganges basin supported multiple states and influential philosophical and religious traditions. The exact birth and death chronologies of prominent early religious founders are debated; the pack deliberately does not invent exact lifetime dates. In East Asia, Zhou royal authority coexisted with powerful competing political centres. In Africa, Saharan and Nile societies, Mediterranean Punic networks and early sub-Saharan material traditions developed in distinctive local contexts. American archaeology shows complex trajectories in Oaxaca, Central Mexico, Maya regions and the Andes long before later famous empires.

### 400–301 BCE: Macedonian expansion and new imperial systems

Greek interstate competition and Macedonian political consolidation culminated in Philip II's victory at Chaeronea (338 BCE). Alexander's campaigns (334–330 BCE) ended Achaemenid imperial authority. The death of Alexander in 323 BCE was the start of prolonged successor struggles, not the instantaneous completion of a new settled geopolitical map. At roughly the same moment, Chandragupta and other leaders transformed Magadhan politics into Mauryan imperial authority in South Asia. The connection is contemporaneity and occasional successor-state contact, not a single combined empire.

### 300–201 BCE: Roman–Carthaginian wars, Ashoka and Qin–Han transition

The Ptolemaic and Seleucid monarchies reorganised Mediterranean and West Asian state networks. Rome and Carthage fought the First Punic War (264–241 BCE) and a second war starting in 218 BCE; Numidian royal participation illustrates why Roman and Punic wars cannot be depicted using only two opposing empires. The Mauryan ruler Ashoka left inscriptions that can ground precise studies of royal ideology, governance and religious patronage. In China, Qin unification came in 221 BCE, followed by a short Qin regime and the establishment of Han rule in 206 BCE.

The Eastern and Western Hemispheres were not connected by the exchange networks documented for Afro-Eurasia. Formative societies across Mesoamerica, early South American communities and North American settlement networks should appear **simultaneously**, but not connected through invented diplomatic or maritime routes.

### 200–101 BCE: Han geography, Mediterranean expansion and regional South Asian dynamics

Western Han bureaucracy, material production and local elite burials provide exceptionally detailed evidence. The original Mawangdui silk topographic and garrison maps, buried no later than 168 BCE, are particularly useful for the Atlas's geographically aware past: they reveal a **regional administrative map**, not an ancient global map. Han diplomatic missions to Central Asia beginning in the late 2nd century BCE expanded the Chinese court's reported geographical awareness while relying upon routes and communities that already existed.

Roman influence expanded around the Mediterranean, including in parts of North Africa and Greek-speaking territories. In South Asia, post-Mauryan dynasties, Indo-Greek kingdoms, and regional Deccan powers had overlapping histories and cultural practices. The Americas show independent trajectories, including Zapotec, Maya and Andean settlement and craft traditions.

### 100–1 BCE: Roman constitutional conflict and Hellenistic transitions

In the late Republic, Rome experienced civil wars, political conflicts over citizenship, powerful military commands and social inequalities. The Roman defeat at Carrhae in 53 BCE demonstrates substantial Parthian military strength. The assassination of Julius Caesar (44 BCE) did not restore a stable republican government. After Actium (31 BCE), Ptolemaic royal authority ended in Egypt, which came under Roman rule in 30 BCE. The traditional start of the Principate in 27 BCE is a **political convention**, not the invention of Roman civilisation.

Glassblowing arose in the Syro-Palestinian region in the late first century BCE and later spread very widely through Roman-controlled regions. It is wrong to claim glassblowing was invented in Rome because surviving Roman glass objects are plentiful. Other societies continued to develop independent artistic, religious and craft traditions, without entering a unified global world system.

### 1–100 CE: regional trade and urban cultures

The **Periplus of the Erythraean Sea**, commonly dated to the middle of the first century CE, gives geographically rich testimony concerning the Red Sea, East African ports, Arabia and India. Its primary historical value is evidence for named commercial connections, seasonal sailing practices and commodities. It does **not** demonstrate that Roman emperors knew or controlled every destination described, nor that Rome and China had routine direct diplomatic contact.

Roman expansion continued in Europe and the Near East. Indian Ocean trading contacts overlapped Kushan and Satavahana developments in South Asia. In East Asia, Han institutions supported writing, specialised crafts and diverse livelihoods. Ancient urban centres at Teotihuacan and Monte Albán, early Maya networks, Hopewell ceremonial activity and Nazca-related coastal systems demonstrate the richness of the independently developing American hemisphere.

### 101–200 CE: consolidation, religious and technical transformations

The Roman annexation of Nabataea in 106 CE exemplifies how caravan-linked regional states could become provinces without their inhabitants or languages suddenly disappearing. Roman and Parthian relations combined conflict, frontier politics and diplomatic contact. Paper production in China became more administratively visible during Han times, while the traditional single-inventor narrative about Cai Lun in 105 CE is misleading because earlier archaeological paper exists. Glass vessel manufacture and other technologies circulated through Roman commercial worlds.

Buddhist and other South Asian religious and artistic traditions developed alongside changing regional state institutions. Chinese political and diplomatic records are a rich but elite-skewed source. East African Aksumite and Nile-region evidence remains more spatially and chronologically uneven than Chinese court material, without warranting exclusion from the Atlas.

### 201–300 CE: political reorganisation across Afro-Eurasia

Eastern Han authority ended in 220 CE. Cao Wei, Shu Han and Eastern Wu became major competing political regimes; later Western Jin expanded and reunified large Chinese regions by 280. In Iran, the Sasanians displaced Arsacid imperial power in 224. Rome's third-century crises involved repeated imperial succession, military and economic pressures; they did not amount to a uniform instantaneous imperial collapse.

Aksumite minting in the late third century provides evidence for highland political institutions connected to Red Sea commerce. In Japan the emergent Kofun/Yamato burial and leadership systems require archaeology-led dating, while Korean communities were developing forms of political centralisation and material exchange. Moche and Nasca practices in the Andes coexisted with Teotihuacan urban expansion and Hopewell ceremonial activity in North America, without the evidence implying direct transoceanic contact.

### 301–400 CE: religion, contested frontiers and synchrony

The religious policies associated with Constantine and Licinius in 313 CE altered the legal position of Christianity within the Roman imperial system. That same year, Goguryeo's conquest of the Lelang commandery signalled a separate political transition in Korea. Constantinople became a major imperial capital in 330 CE. Fourth-century Aksumite royal Christianity is supported by inscriptions and changes in coin imagery; it was a royal and institutional process rather than proof that all residents converted simultaneously.

**378 CE is one of the Atlas's strongest world-history discoveries.** Roman forces were defeated near Adrianople, with Emperor Valens killed; in the Maya lowlands, inscriptions connect the same year to Teotihuacan-linked dynastic transformation at Tikal. These were entirely different events in different hemispheres. Displaying them on one aligned timeline is compelling precisely because it does **not** claim influence, communication or any causal relationship. Tikal's exact political mechanism is still a subject of interpretation.

### 401–500 CE: continuing empires, political transition and diverse societies

The political division between eastern and western Roman courts consolidated after 395. Rome was sacked in 410; Vandal power developed in North Africa during the 430s; Romulus Augustulus was deposed in 476. **Only the western imperial office ended in 476. The Eastern Roman state did not disappear**, and post-Roman Italian governments interacted with Roman institutional legacies. In 493, Theoderic established Ostrogothic kingship in Italy.

Around this era, a ruler founded a dynastic line at Copán in 427 CE, Goguryeo captured a major Baekje capital in 475 CE, and Japanese high-fired pottery traditions were increasingly linked to Korean peninsula craftsmen. These developments show why a single title such as “the fall of Rome” cannot stand for the global fifth century. Continuity matters as much as political succession.

**Regional balance is a research objective, not an artificial quota.** Europe, East Asia and West Asia supply comparatively dense historical writing. North and South America, Oceania, the Arctic and Africa frequently require different archaeology-led methods. Some regional records are intentionally broad archaeological categories, not sovereign states. The **153 count cannot be interpreted as 153 empires or 153 equally documented civilisations.**

### Africa and the Mediterranean

The Nile Valley, Egypt, Kush/Meroe, Nubian cultural traditions and northeast African states were not simply external dependencies of Rome. Aksum's early commercial contacts and minting belong in their own Red Sea and highland histories. Carthage, Numidia, Mauretania and Roman African provincial transformations document more of western North African political sequence. In the Sahara, Garamantian oasis engineering and communications are an important alternative to assuming empty desert. West African Nok-related material traditions and Jenne-jeno settlement are especially underrepresented in narrative sources; their dates are archaeological ranges, not dynastic regnal lists. Source leads: [The Met, Western North Africa](https://82nd-and-fifth.metmuseum.org/toah/ht/04/afw.html), [The Met, African art](https://www.metmuseum.org/-/media/files/learn/for-educators/publications-for-educators/the-art-of-africa.pdf), [UNESCO Aksum](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/15).

### West, South and Central Asia

Achaemenid, Hellenistic successor, Parthian and Sasanian polities overlapped extensive local systems, not only the wars between great capitals. Arabia and the Levant contained caravan-linked states, local kingdoms and trade networks. The Iranian plateau and Mesopotamian political sequence is one of the strongest temporal spines, but exact borders and ethnic group labels need much more caution. South Asia includes Magadhan dynasties, Mauryan imperial rule, Indo-Greek, Kushan, Satavahana, Western Kshatrapa, Gupta, Sri Lankan and regional Tamil polities. Buddhism, Brahmanical traditions, local ritual and craft systems cannot be mapped as instantaneous empire-wide conversions. Source leads: [The Met, Iran](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/toah/ht/05/wai), [The Met, South Asia](https://www.metmuseum.org/ko/toah/ht/05/ssa), [UNESCO Silk Roads](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1442).

### East Asia and island Southeast Asia

The late Zhou Warring States and Qin–Han transformations are important for demonstrating fine-grained political succession. Han society cannot be treated as one unchanged empire for more than 400 years; Western Han, Wang Mang's Xin, Eastern Han and the subsequent rival states need distinct timeline observations. Korea's Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla and Gaya traditions require differentiation between traditional origin legends and archaeological consolidation. Yayoi and Kofun are archaeological periods, not Japanese emperors or single states. Mainland Southeast Asian Dong Son, Sa Huynh, early Mekong port systems and 'Funan' textual labels cannot be converted automatically into exact territorial polygons. Source leads: [The Met, China](https://www.metmuseum.org/zh/toah/ht/05/eac.html), [The Met, Korea](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/toah/ht/05/eak), [The Met, Japan](https://www.metmuseum.org/ja/toah/ht/05/eaj).

### Americas, Arctic and Caribbean

Teotihuacan, Monte Albán, Maya city networks, Hopewell exchange and ceremonial landscapes, and Hohokam and Puebloan precursors illustrate independent innovation outside Afro-Eurasian exchange. The Americas contain multiple time-dependent political and archaeological layers; an “Olmec to Maya to Aztec” linear civilisational progression would be false. South American archaeology includes Paracas, Nasca, Moche, Recuay, Pukara, early Tiwanaku, and northern Andean ceramic traditions; none should be promoted to a single homogenous Andean empire. In the Caribbean, broad ceramic traditions are a research starting point rather than an invented single tribal polity. Paleo-Inuit, Bering Sea and related Arctic traditions similarly require marine-adaptation and archaeological chronology rather than dynastic assumptions. Source leads: [UNESCO Teotihuacan](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/414), [UNESCO Tikal](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/64), [UNESCO Hopewell](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1689), [The Met, Andean chronology](https://www.metmuseum.org/ko/toah/ht/05/sac).

### Australia and Oceania

Australia was continuously inhabited throughout this interval, but no single Australian polity or universal Aboriginal boundary can be responsibly constructed. The Gunditjmara Budj Bim cultural landscape has ancient aquaculture evidence, yet this is **not** a warrant to date one specific structure to a fabricated year such as 100 CE. Indigenous cultural knowledge and its display rights have independent safeguards. Western Polynesian and Melanesian settlement and voyaging traditions are relevant; remote Eastern Polynesian settlements such as Aotearoa, Hawai‘i and Rapa Nui must not be backdated into this period on the basis of outdated chronologies. This pack supplies no invented map of Aboriginal Country. Sources: [UNESCO Budj Bim](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1577), [AIATSIS mapping caveats](https://aiatsis.gov.au/explore/map-indigenous-australia), [Wilmshurst et al., radiocarbon reassessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3033267/).

## 4. Technological knowledge — invention, evidence, use, diffusion

The **63 dated technology development observations** are not 63 invention claims. They distinguish `earliest_attested_evidence`, `documented_use`, `adoption`, `expanded_network`, `iconographic_improvement`, `documented_diplomacy`, and other historical statuses. An invention can precede the earliest surviving artefact. The timeline must never translate all statuses into “invented by society X in year Y.”

| Development | Evidence in this research window | Historical caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Ironworking | Warring States Chinese iron, Roman tools, South Asian manufacturing and changing African evidence | Older iron is known in other regions; surviving items do not prove global origin |
| Glassblowing | Syro-Palestinian late-1st-century BCE appearance, then wider Roman adoption | Roman mass production ≠ Roman invention; mould-blowing a separate later refinement |
| Paper | Early Chinese papermaking evidence pre-dates the Cai Lun attribution of 105 CE | 105 is a reported refinement and court association, not reliable zero-point invention date |
| Coinage | Hellenistic, Punic, Roman and Aksumite issue traditions | Currency functions include state symbolism and not necessarily modern retail cash |
| Roads, concrete, aqueducts | Roman and local provincial engineering through BCE and CE periods | Itiner-e shows many specific routes are conjectural in location |
| Cartography | Han original silk topographic and military maps buried by 168 BCE | They depict a local region, not the known world of every Han resident |
| Indian Ocean sailing | Practical seasonal-wind routes described by the Periplus | Text reflects commercial knowledge, not every sailor's experience |
| Mesoamerican arts and mathematics | Maya writing, calendars, Teotihuacan obsidian industries | Independent development; no Roman or Chinese connection implied |
| North American engineering | Hopewell geometric earthworks, regional irrigated farming | Monumental work is not proof of one unified empire |
| Andean textiles, adobe and land art | Paracas/Nasca/Moche and related archaeological traditions | Chronologies vary by site; similar construction doesn't prove diffusion |

The project is explicitly designed to support an interactive comparative technology tree with **technique, society, evidence, dates, confidence, source and contextual narrative**, rather than a superficial single global first-use table. Primary sources or museum object records that require further publication rights review should be linked, not embedded as copied images.

## 5. Geography and the world's known horizons

A geographical knowledge map must be different from a modern atlas with old country names. The data include **5 historical cartographic catalogue entries** and **7 geographical-awareness research records**. Three Han silk-map items are described as ancient original or fragmentary objects, while the Ptolemaic coordinate-geography tradition and Tabula Peutingeriana are identified as materials surviving in **much later copy or reconstruction**. Date of composition, extant physical object and digital reproduction must remain separate.

Mawangdui's **topographic map** and **garrison map** were entombed before 168 BCE and demonstrate sophisticated regional representation. These maps show specific administrative and military spaces, not the entire world known to China. [Archaeology Magazine](https://archaeology.org/issues/may-june-2019/collection/maps-china-han-dynasty-topographic-map/mapping-the-past/) describes the surviving silk maps. The *Periplus* supports an alternative kind of historical map: a text-derived network of Red Sea and Indian Ocean routes with named ports and temporal sailing patterns. A visually impressive reconstruction should remain labelled a reconstruction. The *Tabula Peutingeriana* likewise contains ancient route traditions but is not preserved as an original Roman-era object; the surviving manuscript is medieval. [UNESCO catalogue](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/tabula-peutingeriana?hub=1081).

One recent scholarly network dataset offers an especially strong warning. **Itiner-e** digitises approximately 299,000 km of Roman roads, but reports that only a small percentage of its network is mapped with high positional certainty. It can support map layers, provided segment uncertainty and data licence are carried over. [Itiner-e, Scientific Data, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-025-06140-z). **Pleiades** is a scholarly gazetteer for historical place IDs; **ORBIS** models Roman transport cost rather than proving every route's precise linework. These are complementary modern tools, not original historical maps.

The accompanying `geo/historical_place_anchors.geojson` has 30 modern point locators such as Carthage, Mawangdui, Pataliputra, Teotihuacan, Tikal, Hopewell and Tiwanaku. All are labelled as **reference points**, not historical polity cores or boundaries. No artificial borders or confident transoceanic routes are drawn. Layers named 'attested knowledge', 'inferred awareness' and 'unsupported' must be visually distinct.

## 6. Historical relationships and “Meanwhile, elsewhere” connections

The relationship graph includes **41 typed and time-scoped links**. Examples are Greek–Achaemenid conflicts; Rome–Carthage wars; Seleucid–Mauryan diplomatic frontier interaction; Han–Xiongnu diplomacy and warfare; Red Sea maritime commerce involving Roman Egypt and ports farther south; Korean–Japanese ceramic-technology exchanges; Roman–Sasanian conflict; and Teotihuacan–Tikal political influence as a disputed interpretation of inscriptions. Different relationship types can coexist between the same actors. A traded object does not prove a state-to-state treaty; a shared ceramic style does not automatically prove conquest.

The **12 simultaneous comparison cards** are different records. Their links mean *comparable in time*, not *historically connected*. The 378 CE Roman/Maya synchronism is the strongest example; 313 CE Europe/Korea also works. The comparative timeline view should make the distinction explicit using both an event ribbon and a separate documented-contact graph. Unsupported American–Afro-Eurasian contact edges are absent.

## 7. Everyday experiences, families, work and religion

The cultural table contains 25 research-seed entries. Athenian and Roman political systems excluded many women, residents, enslaved people and noncitizens from positions accessible to elites. Urban Roman baths, aqueducts and housing should not imply every rural person had equal access to municipal infrastructure. Han silk weaving, Chinese text records, South Asian Buddhist monastic networks, Moche ceramics, Hopewell ceremonies, Paracas textiles, Dong Son bronze drums and Kofun burial landscapes reveal forms of non-military life. These should become an exploratory Atlas layer rather than remaining a paragraph beneath battle histories.

One common trap is **evidence survivorship**: courts and temples preserved inscriptions that rural homes did not. Women's lives, childhood, labour, farming and domestic life require archaeological and social-history research. The biographical table deliberately does not invent named ancient individuals for societies where surviving evidence does not identify rulers or practitioners.

## 8. Source quality and remaining gaps

The source register has **61 entries**. Of those, **24** had a web result or institutional record reviewed during these connected research passes, **36** are institutional or academic pointers **not individually audited**, and one is the supplied research-seed report. A source's existence is **not** proof that every composite summary citing it is correct. The `source_claims` table currently links a record's summary to sources at **record level**; fine-grained claim-to-page verification remains a later stage. Provisional confidence `HIGH` is an editorial assessment, not a certificate of independent validation. There are **22 explicitly recorded gaps**.

Main concerns are under-sampled Central and West African archaeology, regional Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander evidence and permissions, South American site chronology, early Korea dynastic traditional dates, ambiguity between archaeology and polity labels in Southeast Asia, historical-border and migration-line precision, poorly reconstructed domestic histories, disputed religious biographies, original map media licences and the risk of double-importing year 500 CE.
